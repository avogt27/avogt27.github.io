# Switching to Linux: Prelude

My first computer was my parents' Gateway 2000 - I'm not sure what OS was originally installed on it, but I vividly remember switching to the nightmare that was Windows Mellenium Edition, although it worked well enough for us - so long as whatever computer games I wanted were supported (throwback to going to the store and scouring the back of the box of Lego Racers 2 for the big ME to let me know I could play it). At some point in highschool, my tech savy uncle showed off his Macbook Pro and concvinced my folks to make the switch to Apple, some alien looking widescreen setup who's conspicuous lack of a tower made us feel like we were dealing with some sort of futuristic tech. Later I was able to convince my folks that I needed a Macbook Pro (college was coming up after all), which I later upgrated to a higher powered machine that has served all of my needs through one and a half undergraduate degrees plus most of my PhD. 

All in all, I think Apple was probably the best choice all of those years, and it certainly treated me better than the Microsoft alternative as well as being a gateway drug of sorts into the beautiful world of Linux. However, as I've increasingly fallen down the tech rabit hole, it's become clear that Apple's philosophy does not align with my own, particularly coupled with the role of technology in our lives in the strange world we find ourselves in in the year 2025. At the risk of invoking 'old man yells at cloud', I grew up in a time when computers and related tech were essentially passive things that we could pick up and put down at our leisure. Now,they call out for our attention, always. Instead of our curated experiences, the all knowing Algorithms act like a pantheon of Greek Gods, hiding content from us, censoring our experiences, and putting things in our path for their own purposes. Perhaps this is just one small step in reclaiming some control over the tools I use every day.

### The slow crawl towards Linux ###

My transition to Linux has been about as incremental as humanly possible. First off, it's not lost on me that MacOS is essentially just the heaviest Linux distro with a bunch of proprietary stuff tacked on - so there were some structural features that would always be familiar. Second, anyone involved in research that requires anything above the most trivial amount of computing will inevitably have to learn some basic commands, which I of course did over the course of the first few years of my PhD. Eventually, the command line became less mysterious, and I realized how much better it was at doing certain things than I was pointing and clicking around. 

The real changes began when I started seeing real developers' workflows. Folks with window managers and efficient text editors seemed to be basically living in the future, able to connect their computer with their brain (without the dumb brain-computer interface technology certain companies are developing nowadays). Consequently, I got yabai working on my macbook, downloaded iTerm so i could get that cool transparent effect, and started learning my vim motions. More than anything, I remember being excited to use my computer again; everything was such a chore on a standard setup, but now I was moving quicker, and my computing experience had been sort of gamified. 

### A necessary switch ###

I've been terrified to update the OS on my Macbook ever since it came out that Apple had been screwing with the battery life on older iPhones with their software updates. Over time, I've felt like the constellation of software versions I have just work, and updating any of them could lead to an avallanche of unsupported programs. Slowly but surely, my out of date OS has become a problem, not to mention a bunch of hardware on the computer breaking (the battery is more or less shot and the camera does not work at all - although some may see that as a positive). As the software update notifications have piled up, it's become clear that upgrading to something new is becoming a necessity. Looking at new macbooks brought me no joy. Everything seemed basically the same - maybe a little faster, but what do I care when most of what I do day-to-day can either be done on a 10 year old, out of date machine or I need to login to a computing cluster with a couple dozen GPUs to accomplish? Then the revelation came, spend 1/8th of the money on a refurbished laptop and install Linux. The worst thing that can happen is I learn why we haul these expensive machines with bulky software around for everything.
